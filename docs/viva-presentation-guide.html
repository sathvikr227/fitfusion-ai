Presentation & viva preparation

How to explain FitFusion's ML layer

A demo script, the reasoning behind every design choice, and honest answers to the questions your examiners are most likely to ask — including the first build that had to be thrown away and the targets the rebuild did not hit, which are the ones worth talking about most. Updated 2026-11-09 for the rebuilt models.

01Open with this

Ninety seconds, no jargon. Establish the problem before the solution.

Say this

"FitFusion was already a working fitness app — it generated workout plans with an LLM and tracked what users actually did. But two of its most important decisions were hardcoded. Calories burned came from a fixed lookup table of 42 exercises that falls back to a default for anything it doesn't recognise. And the weekly plan adjustment used three fixed thresholds: below 60% completion reduce the plan, above 85% increase it, otherwise leave it alone."

"Neither can learn. A lookup table can't know that a heavier user burns more per minute of strength work. A fixed threshold can't know that 58% completion means something completely different for someone recovering from an injury than for someone who just skipped the gym."

"So I built five machine learning models to replace and extend those rules, served from a separate Python microservice. The key design constraint was that the app must never break if that service goes down — every single call has the original rule-based logic behind it as a fallback."

That last sentence matters. It shows you thought about production reliability, not just accuracy, and it sets up the strongest moment in the demo (step 6 below).

02The five models in one line each

If you memorise one table, make it this one. Every model was rebuilt in Sept–Nov 2026 on real, openly licensed data after the first build's dataset (Gym Members) turned out to have non-physiological, circular or non-sequential labels.

#ModelDataWhy this algorithm
1Session energy
Calibrated XGBoost + SHAP		Malaga treadmill (840 people), calibrated on WEEE lab calorimetrySmall tabular data with a strong monotone signal (heart rate). Depth-2 trees with monotone constraints, and SHAP explains every prediction. Keytel and then the MET table are fallbacks.
2Hybrid recommender
Popularity + ALS + TF-IDF		Food.com, 231,637 recipes, 1.13M ratings; free-exercise-db (876 exercises)Collaborative filtering (ALS) plus content similarity (TF-IDF), on top of a strong popularity prior. Dietary and calorie filters run first and the score can never override them.
3Injury risk

4Trajectory
PyTorch Transformer		Strava logs, 34,753 athletes, 1.09M windowsReads the last 4 run-days as an ordered sequence and predicts whether 10 km-equivalent pace will improve, plateau or regress.
5Adaptive agent
PPO reinforcement learning		Simulator calibrated on Strava, runners and SoccerMon dataReplanning is a decision with delayed consequences. PPO is stable on a 3-action discrete space. All its numbers are simulated.
If asked "why five models and not one?"

"Because these are five different problem types. Predicting energy is regression. Picking meals is retrieval. Injury risk is a calibrated probability. Pace trend is sequence classification. And choosing next week's load, whose payoff arrives later, is sequential decision-making. One model would force the wrong tool onto four of them."

"Heart rate carries most of the signal: removing it drops R² from 0.77 to 0.08. Without heart rate or height the app says so and falls back to Keytel or the MET table, and the response records the reason."

4

Home and Analytics — injury risk and trajectory

The Injury risk from training load card on Home shows the model's relative risk (for example "1.2× the average injury rate") and the four rule-based regions. Analytics shows the Transformer's arrow and sparkline. Both read the same /api/ml-insights route, so they can never disagree.

Say this

"The overall number is a calibrated probability from real runners' injuries, shown relative to the base rate. The regions are rules, and the card says so. I did not invent per-region probabilities, because no open dataset can train them."

5

The weekly check-in — three models in one request

Trigger the weekly check-in, then show the terminal where the pipeline runs in order.

Say this

"One read of the user's week feeds injury risk, then the trajectory, then the RL state. The PPO agent picks reduce, maintain or increase, with a safety mask: never increase while injured or on under 5.5 hours of sleep. Then code applies the decision deterministically — the ±15% intensity change, removing exercises that load flagged regions, and a rest day — and the decision record is saved with the plan."

6

Kill the ML service on purpose

Press Ctrl+C in Terminal 1. Reload the app. Everything still works. Restart it, reload again, and the models come back.

Say this

"Every ML call passes a fallback. If the service is down, slower than 2 seconds, unauthorised or malformed, the rule-based logic runs and the user never sees an error. The live site runs exactly like this today."

05How to talk about what didn't work

This is the difference between a good grade and a great one. Lead with these.

The instinct to hide a missed target is the wrong one. An examiner who finds a weakness you did not mention concludes you do not understand your own system. You have evidence for every one of these.

The first build's dataset was unusable, so I rebuilt everything

Say this

"My first five models used the Gym Members dataset and the numbers looked great — injury F1 of 1.0. When I audited it, yoga burned the same calories per hour as HIIT, the injury labels were rules over the model's own inputs, so F1 = 1.0 was circular, and the 'sequences' were unrelated rows. I retired all five and rebuilt on real lab, recipe, injury and Strava data. The numbers are lower and they are real."

The energy model only ties the published Keytel equation

Say this

"Out of the box XGBoost beat Keytel, but once both were calibrated on lab data they tied: 1.27 against 1.29 kcal/min, p = 0.16. I serve XGBoost because the project specifies an explainable model and SHAP gives per-prediction explanations — and I document the tie rather than claim a win."

Injury: per-region F1 cannot be measured, and the overall signal is weak

Say this

"No open dataset labels knee, back, shoulder and hamstring injuries, so regions are an explicit rule table. The overall model passed its pre-registered bar at AUC 0.595, but it does not significantly beat 'sessions last week'. I also caught a leak before training: the day file has no rows in the three weeks before any injury, so any history feature would have identified injuries by the gap. I stopped, amended the protocol, and used the week file instead."

Trajectory accuracy is 48%; the target was 65%

Say this

"On three balanced classes chance is 33%, and the Transformer beats every baseline on 5,214 held-out athletes, stable across four seeds. Pace on Strava also moves with terrain, weather and effort choice, which caps how predictable it is."

The RL agent failed its pre-registered rule and is served by my override

Say this

"Rounds one and two always learned to push users until they quit. That was a training bug, not PPO: week 26 was marked as truncated, so the library valued surviving users as if they trained forever, and with a slightly negative weekly reward, quitting looked optimal. Fixing that, PPO beats every baseline — but it has slightly more injuries per user than the rule, which fails my pre-registered criterion. Per week trained the injury rate is the same: PPO keeps users who would otherwise quit. I chose to serve it as a documented override, and the metadata records both the rule's verdict and my decision. All of it is simulated."

06Questions you should expect

Answers written the way you would actually say them out loud.

Why a separate Python service? Why not do it all in the web app?

Because the models need Python — XGBoost, PyTorch and stable-baselines3 have no real JavaScript equivalent. Separating them also means the ML layer can be deployed and updated independently, and gives a clean failure boundary: if the Python service dies, the app keeps running on its rule-based logic.

How do the two services authenticate?

A shared bearer token in an environment variable. User identity is established in the web app with the Supabase JWT; the ML service only checks the service token. It fails closed: with no key configured it returns 503 rather than allowing everything through.

Where does the RL agent get its training data?

From a simulator I calibrated on real data: fitness versus training load from Strava, injury risk versus load from the runners dataset, sleep from SoccerMon. Adherence and churn are labelled assumptions and stress-tested at ±25%. Every RL number I quote is simulated, and I say so.

Isn't the simulated environment just telling the agent what you want it to learn?

That risk is real, which is why the protocol was committed before training, validation and test seeds were fresh, and the agent had to beat simple baselines. I also found the simulator's original seven injury bins were exploitable — a planner gamed the noise — so round 3 uses a smooth injury curve.

Are these real injury predictions? Could someone get hurt trusting this?

The overall score is learned from real recorded injuries in runners, but it is a weak, population-level signal, not a diagnosis. Regions are rules. Every response carries a disclaimer shown in the UI, and the system's actions are conservative — remove exercises, add rest, never increase load while injured.

What happens to a brand-new user with no history?

Each model degrades explicitly. Meals fall back to popularity (cold start). Injury risk needs 21 days, otherwise the overall score is null and the response lists what was imputed. Non-runners get a completion-slope rule for trajectory, marked stub: true with a reason. Nothing silently invents data.

How do you know the LLM actually followed the ML decision?

I don't trust it to. The action goes into the prompt, but after the LLM returns the plan, code applies the decision again — intensity change, removal of exercises that load flagged regions, rest day. For meals, the route checks every meal in the plan is one of the recommended recipes, so calories are real.

How did you validate it end to end?

263 automated tests on the ML service, including regression tests for every dietary-flag miss found in live testing, 16 web tests, a clean TypeScript check, and five teacher demos whose outputs are byte-identical on re-run.

What would you do next?

Host the ML service so the live site uses the models instead of fallbacks, collect real in-app logs to validate the trajectory and injury models on FitFusion users, and find or collect region-labelled injury data so the rule table can be replaced.

08Close with this

Say this

"To summarise: five models, each solving a different class of problem, integrated into the exact routes where those decisions used to be hardcoded. Each is trained on real, openly licensed data, evaluated on a protocol committed before training, and reported as measured — including a tie, two missed slide targets, and one model served by a documented override. And the whole layer is optional by design — if the ML service disappears, the app degrades to exactly what it was before, with no errors and no impact on the user."

That closing does three things at once: it summarises, it is honest about the limits, and it ends on the engineering decision you are most likely to be given credit for.
